In-text citations in opinions of the U.S. Supreme Court follow specific formatting guidelines designed to ensure consistency and clarity. These guidelines are detailed in the "Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation," which is the most commonly used citation manual in U.S. legal writing. Here’s an overview of the key formatting practices for in-text citations in Supreme Court opinions:

### Key Formatting Guidelines for In-Text Citations

1. **Case Names**:
   - Case names are typically italicized when mentioned in-text and in citations.
   - For example: *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803).

2. **Citation Form**:
   - The full case citation includes the case name, volume number, reporter abbreviation, first page of the case, and the year of the decision.
   - Example: *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954).

3. **Pinpoint Citations**:
   - When citing a specific page within a case, include the page number after the full citation.
   - Example: *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954).

4. **Prior and Subsequent History**:
   - Citations may include information about prior and subsequent proceedings.
   - Example: *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), reh'g denied, 385 U.S. 890 (1966).

5. **Quotations**:
   - When quoting text from a case, provide the page number from which the quote is taken.
   - Example: *Roe v. Wade*, 410 U.S. 113, 153 (1973) (“This right of privacy . . . is broad enough to encompass a woman’s decision whether or not to terminate her pregnancy.”).

6. **Use of Parentheticals**:
   - Parentheticals may be used to provide additional information about the case, such as the court’s decision or to clarify the relevance of the case.
   - Example: *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 561 (1995) (holding that the Gun-Free School Zones Act exceeded Congress's Commerce Clause authority).

7. **Short Form Citations**:
   - After the first full citation, cases can be cited using a shortened form, especially if cited again within the same document.
   - Example: After citing *Brown v. Board of Education* in full, it may be referred to as *Brown*, 347 U.S. at 495.

8. **String Citations**:
   - Multiple cases or authorities can be cited together, separated by semicolons.
   - Example: *Roe v. Wade*, 410 U.S. 113, 153 (1973); *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey*, 505 U.S. 833, 846 (1992).

9. **Formatting Statutes and Other Legal Materials**:
   - Statutes, regulations, and other legal documents have specific citation formats.
   - Example: 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (2012).

### Example of In-Text Citations in a Supreme Court Opinion

**Example Text from a Hypothetical Supreme Court Opinion:**

The Court’s decision in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), fundamentally altered the landscape of American education. As noted in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), separate educational facilities had been deemed permissible. However, *Brown*, 347 U.S. at 495, overturned this precedent, recognizing that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."

In subsequent decisions, such as *Grutter v. Bollinger*, 539 U.S. 306 (2003), the Court has continued to grapple with the implications of *Brown* for modern educational policy.

**Explanation:**

- *Brown v. Board of Education* is cited with the full citation upon first mention.
- In subsequent references, a short form (*Brown*, 347 U.S. at 495) is used.
- *Plessy v. Ferguson* is cited with its full citation to provide context.
- A quote is provided from *Brown*, with the page number where the quote can be found.
- Later cases that discuss or are related to *Brown* are cited to show the continuing impact.

### Summary

In-text citations in Supreme Court opinions must follow the meticulous rules outlined by the Bluebook to maintain precision and uniformity. This practice allows readers to quickly locate sources and understand the context of cited authorities.
